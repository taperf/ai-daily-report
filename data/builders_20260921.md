### 7. When AI Improves Itself | Richard Socher (Recursive) — The MAD Podcast with Matt Turck

**The Takeaway:** Anything you can simulate, AI will solve — and the next frontier is pointing that machinery at science itself, starting with AI doing AI research.

Richard Socher is one of the most cited researchers in AI — the man behind prompt engineering (DecaNLP), word vectors, and a stint as chief scientist at Salesforce — and he now runs Recursive, a company built for one purpose: recursive self-improvement, AI that makes better AI. He just raised $650 million for it, committed roughly $410 million of that to a single compute deal with Amazon, and his new book, The Eureka Machine, lands September 22.

His starting point is contrarian: scientific progress has slowed down. Human knowledge has gone from a body to a labyrinth — 34,000 journals that "might as well have no trespassing signs" — and no single person can span the subfields anymore. AI, he argues, is what calculus was to physics: the tool that weaves the pieces back together. The engine has four pillars — LLMs ingesting human knowledge, scientific measurements beyond human perception, simulation, and robotic labs collecting real-world data — with an agent swarm on top.

His most quotable rule: "Anything you can simulate, AI will solve." That covers games, then math ("the whole field will change massively within the next few years"), then programming — software is eating the world, AI is eating software. Biology is harder because we can't yet simulate a cell, let alone an organ; the bottleneck is data, not algorithms. On hallucination, he flips the frame: for exploring novel proteins, "how well can you hallucinate?" is the right question — it's a feature, not a bug. He rejects the hard-takeoff thesis (physics and clinical trials take time), predicts AI will meaningfully help cure multiple cancers, and invokes Jevons paradox for jobs: coding got cheaper, demand for programmers went up. On policy simulations at Salesforce, his AI economist beat the field's standard optimal-taxation baseline — he hopes someone gives it its "GPT-3 moment." His parting frame: we are nowhere near the upper bounds of any of the ten spaces of intelligence.

**核心结论：** 凡是可以模拟的，AI 终将解决——而下一个前沿是把这套机器对准科学本身，从「用 AI 做 AI 研究」开始。

Richard Socher 是 AI 领域被引用最多的研究者之一——prompt engineering（DecaNLP）、词向量的提出者，曾任 Salesforce 首席科学家——如今他创办了 Recursive，一家只做一件事的公司：递归自我改进，让 AI 造出更强的 AI。他为这家公司融了 6.5 亿美元，其中约 4.1 亿美元签给了亚马逊作为单一算力协议，他的新书《The Eureka Machine》9 月 22 日上市。

他的出发点是一个反直觉判断：科学进步正在变慢。人类知识已经从「一个整体」变成「一座迷宫」——3.4 万本期刊「形同竖着禁止入内的牌子」——没有人再能横跨所有子领域。他主张，AI 之于生物学，就像微积分之于物理学：是把碎片重新编织回整体的工具。这台「尤里卡机器」有四大支柱——摄取人类知识的 LLM、超越人类感知的科学测量、模拟环境、采集真实世界数据的机器人实验室——顶端再叠加一个 agent 集群。

他最值得引用的定律是：「凡是可以模拟的，AI 终将解决。」这覆盖了游戏，接着是数学（「整个领域将在未来几年发生巨变」），然后是编程——软件正在吃掉世界，AI 正在吃掉软件。生物学更难，因为我们还无法模拟一个细胞，更别说器官；瓶颈在数据，不在算法。关于幻觉，他反转了框架：在探索全新蛋白质时，正确的问题是「你的幻觉能有多好？」——幻觉是特性，不是缺陷。他反对「硬起飞」论（物理规律和临床试验需要时间），预测 AI 将切实帮助治愈多种癌症，并用杰文斯悖论解释就业：编程变便宜了，程序员需求反而上升了。在 Salesforce 期间，他的「AI 经济学家」在模拟社会中给出的税收政策建议击败了领域公认的最优税制基线——他希望有人能给它一次「GPT-3 时刻」。他的收尾判断：在十个智能空间里，我们离任何一个的上限都还很远。
