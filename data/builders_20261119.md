# PODCASTS

## Latent Space — Building Visual Intelligence — Andreas Blattmann & Dustin Podell, Black Forest Labs

> **The Takeaway:** Image generation and robot action prediction turned out to be mathematically the same problem, and the team that built latent diffusion is betting a robot should learn an action after seeing it once, which they expect to land in 2027.

Black Forest Labs 的两位核心 Andreas Blattmann（Andi）和 Dustin Podell，一个是从海德堡时代就做扩散模型、latent diffusion 的共同发明人，此前是 SDXL 与 SD3 后训练的核心；另一个是做了十年软件开发的工程师，2022 年 Stable Diffusion 发布当天辞了职，一路做到模型与研究负责人，SDXL、Control LoRA、Flux Ultra 和 Flux Context 都出自他手。他们现在在做的事，是把「生成图像」这个业务当成通往世界模型的途中站——按 Andi 的说法，BFL 成立时就不是为了做一家图像生成实验室，视频建模才是理解世界运作方式的中心手段。公司累计融资 4.5 亿美元，去年 12 月公开的收入是 1 亿美元，Andi 确认「此后已经越过这个数」。

最有价值的不是历史，是他们怎么解释技术选择的理由。SD3 那篇论文的核心问题听上去很朴素：从数据到噪声这条轨迹上，模型到底在哪一段学到了东西，算力应该花在哪一段？他们的答案是，均匀采样会在不重要的一段上浪费大量训练算力，需要对信息量大的区间过采样。从经典 diffusion 迁移到 rectified flow，Andi 给的理由同样是把问题简化：diffusion 要求两个分布中必须有一个是标准正态，flow matching 不需要这个限制，数学上更一般，采样也更快。同样的逻辑用在 Flux Context 上，Dustin 的论证是模型已经学到了足够多的空间关系知识，只要有正确的数据和对预训练模型的正确训练方式，就能用很少的训练量唤出通用的图像编辑能力。

真正让人意外的一个实验结论是 3D。他们证明了只用普通 2D 视频预训练的模型，本身就学到了一定量的 3D 理解，在 3D 数据上做大约 1000 步微调就能得到一个 3D 模型。换句话说，世界理解可以从平面视频里长出来，你只需要给它一点方向上的聚焦。这大概是整期播客里最反直觉、也最能说明「视频建模是中心手段」这条论断的地方。

关于评测，Dustin 说得很直白：「play with your models」，也就是先自己去玩模型。他们公开的 Context Bench 有一半来自深夜在办公室里互相丢图片、输入最蠢的 prompt，理由是「你得让人玩得开心」。Andi 补了一句更实用的：任何评测都有盲区，要把尽可能多独立评测拼起来看，但这不能免除你自己去采样模型、判断它强项和弱项的责任，「这是开发模型的核心部分」。

前瞻部分是机器人。他们的 Flux.3 是单一模型，同时预测世界状态和下一个动作，现在跑在大概 10 FPS，每次预测 9 到 21 帧的动作块，真实机器人和仿真里都能用；computer use 被他们看作同一映射在数字世界的版本。Andi 明确说他们不打算主要靠 fine-tuning，而是要把上下文学习做到极致：现在的标准是四小时示范数据，目标是让机器人「看一次任何动作，然后就能做」——「那才是真正的超人类视觉智能」。这些精细动作现在还要花数周甚至数月去学。他的时间点是 2027 年：消费品层面「下半年会有些相当有意思的机器人进家门」，制造业「有很大机会看到大量应用被解锁」。他们也给这条路线留了一个诚实的余地：机器人受物理制造约束，模型能扩展的东西不一定能同样速度落地。

The most useful part is not the history but how they justify technical choices. The core question behind the SD3 paper sounds almost naive: along the trajectory from data to noise, which segment is the model actually learning from, and where should compute go? Their answer is that uniform sampling wastes training compute on segments that do not matter, so the informative regions should be oversampled. Moving from classic diffusion to rectified flow gets a similar simplification argument from Andreas Blattmann: diffusion requires one of the two distributions to be a standard normal, flow matching does not, so it is more general mathematically and samples faster. The same logic underwrites Flux Context, where Dustin Podell argues the model already carries enough spatial knowledge that the right data and the right training of a pretrained model can evoke general image editing with very little training.

The surprising result is about 3D. They showed that a model pretrained only on plain 2D video already carries a substantial amount of 3D understanding, enough that roughly 1,000 fine-tuning steps on 3D data yields a 3D model. World understanding, in other words, can grow out of flat video if you give it a small amount of directional focus. That is probably the most counterintuitive finding here and the strongest evidence for the claim that video modeling is the central means.

On evaluation, Dustin Podell is blunt: play with your models. Half of their public Context Bench came from late nights in the SF office throwing images at each other and typing the stupidest prompts, because the models need to be fun to use. Andreas Blattmann adds the more practical point: every eval has blind spots, so combine as many independent ones as possible, but that does not remove your own responsibility to sample the model and find its strengths and weaknesses. That is a core part of developing a model.

The forward-looking part is robotics. Their Flux.3 is a single model predicting both world state and next action, running at roughly 10 FPS with chunked predictions of 9 to 21 frames, working on real robots and in simulation, with computer use treated as the same mapping in the digital world. Andreas Blattmann says they explicitly do not want to lean on fine-tuning but instead push context learning to the limit: four hours of demonstration data is the current standard, and the goal is a robot that sees any action once and can then do it, which he calls genuinely superhuman visual intelligence. Those fine motor tasks still take weeks to months of learning today. His date is 2027, with some genuinely useful robots reaching homes in the back half of that year and a strong chance that manufacturing applications unlock well before consumer ones. They leave one honest caveat on the table: robotics is bound by physical manufacturing constraints, so what scales in models does not necessarily scale at the same rate in atoms.
