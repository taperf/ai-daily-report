### Box CEO Aaron Levie

Box CEO Aaron Levie spent the week meeting with technology leaders across banking, media, information services, insurance, and consulting. His observations on how enterprises are actually deploying AI are some of the most grounded and detailed field reports you'll find right now.

Key trends he's seeing:

- **Cybersecurity anxiety is real.** The OpenAI-Hugging Face incident has enterprises genuinely nervous about AI-generated vulnerabilities. The concern is operational, not existential — they're scrambling to keep up with patches and changes.

- **Model fragmentation continues.** Most enterprises are running multiple frontier models internally. Too hard to standardize on one, but dollars are still concentrated among a few vendors. Open weights are still in infancy at scale.

- **Agent identity and security.** Post-Hugging Face, there's heightened awareness around agent security and identity management. Enterprises want to set up identities for agents and control their access, but agents sometimes need to act as the user too.

- **Process reengineering over bolt-on.** The biggest ROI comes from redesigning workflows around agents, not just layering agents onto existing flows. The challenge: who inside the org actually drives this change? Best practice so far: embedded full-stack developers within functions.

- **Ruthless vendor switching.** Because innovation is happening so fast, enterprises aren't waiting for vendors to get things right — they just move on. Levie says he's never heard "we tried X and it didn't work so we went with Y" more than in today's environment.

- **Evals are still early.** Only a few companies out of dozens have a solid grasp on evaluating their AI workflows. Huge opportunity for enterprises to actually understand how their work happens and how well AI performs against it.

- **Legacy systems remain the bottleneck.** Data fragmented across legacy environments that weren't built for an agentic world. Companies spending significant time just cleaning up old platforms.

### Boris Cherny, Claude Code @ Anthropic

Boris Cherny shared his framework for managing code quality when using Claude Code at Anthropic. His take: there's room for both "black box" prototype code and production-grade AI-generated code — but they need different standards.

For throwaway prototypes with low blast radius, don't over-engineer. But for production code written by Claude, Anthropic holds it to a *higher* bar than human-written code. Their guardrails include: extensive lint rules, tests, Claude-driven end-to-end tests, daily fuzzers, automated code and security reviews, and automated refactoring.

If Claude's code doesn't meet the bar, his advice: use the latest frontier model (Opus 5 or Fable 5.1), increase effort to high/xhigh, invest in CLAUDE.md and skills files, or — if all else fails — steer Claude more manually or have it rewrite the codebase to reduce accumulated debt.

### Madhu Guru, Sr Director AI @ Meta

Madhu Guru published part 10 of his "How to build great evals" series. His core argument: measure the steps, not just the result.

Two agent trajectories might both produce the same answer (42), but one searched the right sources, retrieved the right document, made 4 clean tool calls, and calculated the result. The other made 17 calls, searched the same thing 3 times, recovered from 2 errors, and eventually got there. The difference matters.

His framework:
1. Clearly define your whole workflow
2. Define the tasks in each step
3. Think through how to measure each step
4. Define median and hard tasks — reflect them in your evals

When reviewing eval results, study the steps first and the final results second.

https://x.com/realmadhuguru/status/2098064969464217720

Madhu Guru发布了"如何构建优秀eval"系列的第10部分。他的核心论点：测量步骤，而不仅仅是结果。

两个agent轨迹可能都得出相同的答案（42），但一个搜索了正确的来源、检索了正确的文档、进行了4次干净的工具调用并计算出结果。另一个进行了17次调用、重复搜索了3次、从2次错误中恢复，最终才到达。差异很重要。

他的框架：
1. 清晰定义整个工作流程
2. 定义每一步的任务
3. 思考如何测量每一步
4. 定义中等难度和困难任务——在eval中反映它们

审查eval结果时，先研究步骤，再研究结果。

### The MAD Podcast with Matt Turck — When AI Improves Itself | Richard Socher (Recursive)

**The Takeaway:** Richard Socher believes we are far from the true upper bounds of intelligence, and recursive self-improvement — AI building better AI — is the key unlock to get there.

Richard Socher, one of the most cited researchers in AI, just raised $650 million for Recursive, a company built to pursue recursive self-improvement (RSI). He also has a new book, *The Eureka Machine*, laying out a blueprint for how AI will revolutionize science.

**Why scientific progress is slowing:** Socher argues that science has fractured into 34,000+ subfields — what he calls a "labyrinth of knowledge." We've gone from a small body of knowledge that Renaissance thinkers could master, to a world where even biologists studying different layers of biology can't talk to each other. Academia also discourages risk-taking; Socher recounts how his early neural network papers for NLP were widely rejected in 2010.

**AI as the unifier:** Socher sees AI as "what calculus did for physics" — the tool that can weave together complex systems we currently only understand in fragments. He gives the example of proteins: we understand individual neurons and synapses, but not how billions of them together produce thought. AI can model these emergent properties.

**Next-token prediction is more powerful than it seems:** The core insight — by predicting the next token, LLMs incidentally learn world models. Predict "I was in New York driving north to ___" and the model learns geography. Apply the same to protein sequences, and you get AlphaFold. Tokenization works for text, proteins, pixels, sound — everything can be discretized and predicted.

**Hallucination as a feature, not a bug:** For creative scientific discovery, hallucination is useful. Higher "temperature" generates tokens further from the training distribution — which is exactly what you want when exploring novel proteins or molecules. For factual queries, you ground with search (what You.com does). For poetry or discovery, you want the model to go beyond what it's seen.

**The four pillars of the Eureka Machine:**
1. **LLMs ingesting human knowledge** — the foundation
2. **Scientific measurements** — expanding beyond what humans can perceive (gravitational waves, gamma rays)
3. **Simulation** — virtual cells, virtual economies, virtual chemistry
4. **Robotic process automation** — running real-world experiments to verify and close the loop

**Recursive's approach:** Start with AI-for-AI research. Build the equivalent of 50,000 PhDs worth of AI research capability. Only then expand to physics, chemistry, and biology. Socher is skeptical of "hard takeoff" — biology still requires real-world trials that take years. But drug discovery is already accelerating: companies now go from one molecular drug in late-stage trials to eight+ different drugs in 6–18 months.

**On jobs and AI:** "If you care about the outputs of an industry, you love AI. If you get paid hourly, you probably hate AI." Socher uses illustrators vs. programmers as examples: illustration demand didn't grow 1000x when price dropped, but coding demand did (Jevons paradox). AI makes entrepreneurs more productive but displaces hourly workers who don't own the IP they create.

**On AI skepticism:** Socher is bullish but pragmatic. "If you want to know why something doesn't work, ask the experts." He cites how NLP experts said one neural network for all tasks was impossible — his DecaNLP paper was rejected as "completely useless." Similar skepticism now exists for AI in biology and medicine. He believes progress will exceed the skeptics but fall short of the hard-takeoff believers.

**Compute is the bottleneck:** Recursive committed $410 million of its $650 million raise to a single compute deal with Amazon. Socher expects this to be "one of the smallest compute deals" in their future.
